Description

JEKray2D is an optical bench simulator that runs in the browser. You place sources, lenses, mirrors and other parts on a virtual breadboard, drag them into position, and the light is traced through them as you move them. You see the result straight away: where the beam goes, where it focuses, what arrives on each screen, and how much power reaches it.

It does more than a basic ray tracer. Each ray carries its polarisation and its optical path, so interferometers show fringes and cavities resonate. A Gaussian beam is followed through every branch of the system. The diffracted field is propagated surface by surface by physical optics propagation, so slits, stops and tight focuses behave as they do on a real bench.

JEKray2D is in open beta. Everything here works and is checked against published results on the validation page, and it is still growing quickly. If something looks wrong, use Report a bug in the tool: it comes straight to us.

Build a bench in minutes

Add parts from the parts picker, by search or by palette, or from the rail beside the bench, and drag or rotate them into place on a 25 mm breadboard grid. Click any part to open its settings: radii, conic and aspheric terms, glass, coatings, reflectivity, wavelength, beam size and more.

Any setting can be swept: set a range and press play to watch the system change as a mirror moves or a lens tilts. The same ranges feed an optimiser, which varies several values at once against several weighted goals, and a Monte Carlo tolerance study.

Share a bench as a link: it opens as you left it, with the same panels pinned, on the same tabs and scrolled to the same place, its sweeps ready to play. Or embed it, live, in a web page or course notes, where readers can drag, zoom and play it.

New to it? The guided tour builds a focusing lens and then a Michelson interferometer with you, a step at a time.

Exact rays through real glass

Rays are traced exactly through spherical, conic and aspheric surfaces, with the refractive index of real glasses (N-BK7, S-LAH64 and others) at each wavelength. Reflection, refraction, total internal reflection, Fresnel losses and thin-film coatings are all included.

Each screen reports spot size, wavefront error, point spread, Strehl ratio, MTF and enclosed energy. In this example you can see why a plano-convex lens should face its curved side to the collimated beam.

The Aberrations tab sweeps a source across the field and reads the system’s aberrations at the image: Seidel’s coefficients (spherical, coma, astigmatism with field curvature, distortion), Zernike terms along the meridian, a spot diagram across the whole field, ray fans, wavefronts, the field curve and distortion.

Waves, not just rays

The field of each beam is propagated through the system, and every surface acts on it with the phase found by the ray trace. Slits, irises, knife edges, beam blocks and masks, whose amplitude and delay you write as a formula, act on the field as well as on the rays.

Branches of the same beam add as fields wherever they meet, so double slits, interferometers and standing waves come out as they should. Zoom in below 50 µm to see the field computed on every pixel.

Cavities and polarisation

When light loops back on itself, JEKray2D finds the cavity for you and reports its free spectral range, finesse, linewidth, stability, Gaussian mode and losses. It also solves the steady-state field and the cavity’s own modes, in the same way as the Fox–Li method.

Put a gain medium inside and it tells you whether the cavity lases and, above threshold, the power it settles at, which leaves the output coupler as a beam on the bench. Polarisation is tracked as complex s and p amplitudes through every mirror, coating, waveplate and polariser, and through birefringent crystals (beam displacers, Wollaston and Glan–Taylor prisms) and Faraday isolators, which pass light one way only. For steep focusing it switches to a full vector calculation of the focal field.

Fibre, free space and the signals between

Draw fibre subsystems next to the free-space optics: lasers and LEDs, couplers, 1×N splitters and star couplers, WDMs, circulators, polarisation beam splitters, Bragg gratings, switches, delay lines, stretchers, tunable filters, depolarisers, semiconductor amplifiers, AOMs and EOMs, joined port to port with fibres you shape by dragging. Their connectors reflect as PC, APC or flat ends do. Fibre tips send light into free space and take it back in by mode overlap, and link planes carry beams between bench tabs, so a large system can be built as subsystems.

Photodiodes and balanced detectors read what real ones would: DC power with dark current and amplifier noise, the RF spectrum of every beat note, the trace on an oscilloscope and a mixer’s output; an optical spectrum analyser shows what is in the fibre. Launches split from one laser interfere, washed out by its coherence length, and an EOM’s sidebands off a cavity give the Pound–Drever–Hall error signal. Fibre amplifiers saturate and add their spontaneous emission, and the photodiodes read its beat noise; atomic vapour cells give the Doppler-broadened and saturated-absorption spectra a laser is locked to. Put a magnetic field on a cell and its lines split into their Zeeman components, worked out exactly from weak fields to the Paschen–Back regime, so DAVLL locks, Faraday filters and polarisation spectroscopy work as on the bench.

Spectra and ultrafast pulses

Every source can carry several wavelengths, so gratings and prisms spread light into its spectrum and chromatic focal shift shows up on its own. Filters and dichroics pass or reflect by wavelength. A source’s coherence is yours to set: a single frequency, a line of its own, or incoherent broadband light such as an LED’s, which adds as powers except near equal paths, where white-light fringes appear.

Give a laser a pulse duration and JEKray2D traces its spectrum through glass, prism and grating compressors, chirped mirrors and fibre. Any screen or photodiode shows the pulse that arrives: its duration against the transform limit, peak power, group delay dispersion and third-order dispersion, spatial chirp and pulse-front tilt.

Nonlinear optics too: periodically poled PPLN and PPKTP crystals double a laser’s frequency (1560 → 780 nm, 1064 → 532 nm) from Boyd and Kleinman’s theory, with the focusing, poling period and temperature tuning and the fundamental used up as it converts. In fibre, strong pulses pick up self-phase modulation, worked out by the nonlinear Schrödinger equation: their spectra broaden, and in anomalous dispersion they can travel as solitons.

Structured light

A source can launch a Hermite–Gauss or Laguerre–Gauss mode, a vortex of any charge, carried as a coherent sum of modes that each keep their own Gouy phase. A pair of cylindrical lenses turns a vortex into a Hermite–Gauss mode, as it does in the lab.

Masks cover what a spatial light modulator shows: spiral phase plates, fork holograms, blazed gratings and any phase you can write as a formula. Rays and beams follow a blazed pattern into its first order, and a mask with detail finer than the field’s grid is flagged rather than quietly smoothed.

Optical traps, atoms to nanoparticles

Put a screen at a focus and its Trap tab reads the trap the light makes for rubidium, caesium, potassium or sodium atoms, or for a dielectric sphere of any glass. It finds where the particle settles, with radiation pressure pushing it along, and gives the depth, the trap frequency in each direction and the light it scatters.

Every beam through the focus counts, as its full vector field: counter-propagating beams make a standing wave, crossed ones a lattice, and a steep focus traps more softly along its polarisation. The particle can sit in vacuum, in air at a set pressure, which gives its damping and quality factor, or in water on the bench.

Electronics and instruments

The signal after the detector has a palette of its own: sources, amplifiers and a transimpedance amplifier, filters, an attenuator, a mixer, a summing junction, a bias tee and a comparator, wired port to port.

Read them as on the bench: an oscilloscope (normal or rolling), a spectrum analyser, a lock-in amplifier and a DAQ, with its sampling, aliasing and quantisation. An optical chopper or a modulated laser puts the signal on the light in the first place.

Paste the answer into JEKray2D with Open ▾ → Pasted text (or Ctrl+V on the bench). If anything could not be used as written, the tool lists it: copy that report back to the assistant and it can fix the file. Check what it built against your source before relying on it.

JEKray2D works in the plane of the bench. It has no skew rays, no out-of-plane tilts and no anamorphic systems, and it does not model scatter, thermal effects or coating dispersion. Power figures ignore absorption unless you set it on a material.

The validation page runs JEKray2D against published results and independent codes, live in your browser. For anything that gets built, funded or published, check the numbers in an established optical design package first.
